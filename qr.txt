# Slide 2: What is a QR Code? (1 minute)

"A QR Code stands for **Quick Response Code**.

It is a two-dimensional barcode that stores information in a machine-readable format.

Unlike traditional barcodes, which store data only horizontally, QR codes store data both horizontally and vertically. Because of this, they can store much more information.

A QR code can contain:

* Website URLs
* Plain text
* Contact information
* Wi-Fi passwords
* Payment links
* And many other types of data.

Almost every smartphone today has a built-in QR scanner, making QR codes very convenient."

---

# Slide 3: Where Are QR Codes Used? (1 minute)

"QR codes are used almost everywhere today.

Some common applications include:

* **Payment links**, such as Google Pay or PhonePe.
* **Restaurant menus**, where customers scan instead of using printed menus.
* **Event tickets**, allowing quick verification.
* **Product information**, providing manufacturing details or instructions.
* **Website URLs**, taking users directly to a webpage.
* **Text messages or instructions**, displaying important information.

Because QR codes are quick and easy to scan, businesses save both time and paper."

# Slide 4: History of QR Code (1 minute)

"QR codes were invented in **1984** by **Denso Wave**, which is part of the Toyota Group in Japan.

Their original purpose was not for payments or websites.

They were designed to **track automobile parts during manufacturing**.

The famous black-and-white square design was inspired by the Japanese board game called **Go**.

Later, because QR codes were efficient and easy to use, they became popular worldwide."

---

# Slide 5: Types of Encoding Modes (1 minute)

"QR codes can store different types of information using different encoding modes.

These include:

* **Numeric Mode** – stores only numbers and supports up to about 7,089 digits.
* **Alphanumeric Mode** – stores numbers, uppercase letters, and some symbols.
* **Byte or Binary Mode** – stores general text and files.
* **Kanji Mode** – specially designed for Japanese characters.

Overall, a QR code can store approximately **3 kilobytes of data**, which is enough for most practical applications."

---

# Slide 6: Anatomy – Finder Pattern (1 minute)

"Now let's understand the parts of a QR code.

The first part is called the **Finder Pattern**.

These are the three large squares located at the top-left, top-right, and bottom-left corners.

Their job is to help the scanner detect:

* where the QR code is,
* its size,
* and its orientation.

Even if the QR code is rotated, these finder patterns help the scanner read it correctly."

---

# Slide 7: Anatomy – Alignment Marker (1 minute)

"The second component is the **Alignment Marker**.

Its purpose is to improve scanning accuracy.

If the QR code is printed on a curved surface, tilted, or viewed from an angle, the alignment marker helps the scanner adjust the image so it can still be read correctly.

Without alignment markers, scanning would be much less reliable."

---

# Slide 8: Anatomy – Timing Pattern (45 seconds)

"The next part is the **Timing Pattern**.

These are alternating black and white modules located between the finder patterns.

Their purpose is to define the grid structure of the QR code.

They tell the scanner exactly where each data square is located."

---

# Slide 9: Anatomy – Version Number (45 seconds)

"Every QR code has a version.

Version information tells the scanner the size of the QR code.

There are **40 versions**.

Version 1 is the smallest QR code, while Version 40 is the largest and can store much more information."

---

# Slide 10: Anatomy – Data Area & Quiet Zone (1 minute)

"The **Data Area** is the most important part because it stores the actual information.

It also includes error correction data.

Around the outside of every QR code is an empty white border called the **Quiet Zone**.

This empty space separates the QR code from the background, allowing scanners to recognize it easily.

Without a proper quiet zone, scanning may fail."

---

# Slide 11: How QR Codes Work Technically (1 minute)

"When we scan a QR code, several technical steps happen automatically.

First, the scanner detects the **finder patterns**.

Next, it reads the **mode indicator** to determine the type of data.

Then it checks the **character count**.

After that, it reads the actual data modules.

Finally, it uses **error correction** to recover any damaged or missing information.

This entire process takes only a fraction of a second."

# Questions the audience may ask

### 1. Why are QR codes better than barcodes?

**Answer:** QR codes store much more information, can be scanned from any direction, and include error correction.

### 2. Which Python library generates QR codes?

**Answer:** The `qrcode` library, along with `Pillow` for image handling.

### 3. Can QR codes work without the internet?

**Answer:** Yes. If the QR code contains text, contact information, or Wi-Fi credentials, it works offline. Internet is only needed if it stores a website URL.

### 4. What is error correction?

**Answer:** It allows a QR code to be read even if part of it is damaged or covered.

### 5. Why is the Quiet Zone important?

**Answer:** It separates the QR code from the background so scanners can detect it correctly.

### 6. What is the maximum storage capacity of a QR code?

**Answer:** Around **3 KB** of data, depending on the encoding mode and QR version.
